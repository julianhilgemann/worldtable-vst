How to read the day strip

It is a keogram, the plot auroral observatories make from their all-sky cameras: one thin north-to-south slice of the sky for every moment, laid side by side. Time runs left to right. The top edge is the northern horizon, the middle is straight overhead, the bottom edge is the southern horizon. The line underneath is the magnetometer.

10–11 May 2024 at College, Alaska, as the Sky Model replays it. Drawn from the same values the dome uses, so the two always agree.

What changed in this pass

Your arrangement

The sky's data sits small beside the keogram. Resonators top left. Harmony as it was. Macros under the dome, squished to one row. Effects along the bottom: phaser and chorus on the left, delay, reverb and output on the right.

An EQ in place of the filter

Four bands. Drag a dot sideways for frequency, up and down for gain, scroll for width. Under each dot a bar: pull it up to drive only that part of the sound. The sky still opens the high cut; the bar on the top edge shows how far.

LFO and envelope

Two LFOs with four shapes, scrolling under a fixed "now". An envelope with three points to drag; it fires on a substorm onset, on a note, or from the Fire button. Both are sources like the macros.

One modulation grid

Every source against every destination, behind the Modulation button. Drag a dot and a ring appears on that knob at once; in the pictures the live value is a white dot.

Two placements I chose

The EQ went under the resonators: it needs the width, and generator then tone reads top to bottom. The LFO and envelope went beside the macros: everything that moves things is one block under the dome. Phaser and chorus took the freed width.

Kept from before

The keogram and transport, the crossover bands, the colour rule (red, green and violet only for the bands), the hint line, the sizes and the tour.
